# Deck outline: "Who Owns Corporate Money Movement?"
### An outside-in point of view on the evolution of corporate money movement. 13 slides, ~18 min plus discussion

Delivery note: this is an independent point of view on the industry, offered for discussion, not a
plan for anyone. Keep the tone curious and opinionated but not dogmatic. Leave 10+ minutes for
conversation; the tensions and open-questions slides are the real invitation.

Five-minute version: the argument answers five questions in order — where is corporate money movement
going (slide 2), why now (slide 3), how does it work as one flow (slides 6–8), where does the value
concentrate (slides 4–5 and 10), and why it matters (slides 11–13).

---

### Slide 1. Title
**Who Owns Corporate Money Movement?**
*An outside-in point of view on the evolution of corporate money movement*
One line under the title: *company-agnostic; a starting point for discussion, not a roadmap.*

---

### Slide 2. The thesis
**Corporate payments is shifting from issuing an instrument to orchestrating the customer's money
movement, and the open question is who ends up owning that orchestration layer.**

Visual: a stack. Bottom is "instrument in a workflow." Middle is "which rail?" Top is "orchestrate the
whole financial workflow." The unit of value is climbing.

---

### Slide 3. Why now: four structural forces (no market-size slide)
- **Multi-rail by default.** Real-time rails carry structured data (ISO 20022); adoption intent is
  broad. Rail selection becomes a product surface.
- **The workflow consolidates around software.** Pay, get paid, forecast, reconcile without leaving
  the system of record.
- **Decisioning moves into the transaction.** Value shifts from the record to the decision that
  produced it — rail, funding source, risk posture, settlement timing.
- **Software starts to act, not just advise.** Agents in finance ops moving from experimentation
  toward infrastructure; a growing share of payment *initiation* stops being a human action.

Takeaway: the center of gravity moves from the instrument to the orchestration of the flow.

---

### Slide 4. The strategic question
**Who owns the intelligent orchestration layer for corporate money movement?**
Five archetypes, each advantaged and constrained:
- Closed-loop financial platforms
- Payment networks
- Banks
- ERP and software platforms
- Fintech infrastructure and orchestrators

---

### Slide 5. Closed-loop: the advantages and the constraints come from the same source
**Advantages:** richest transaction data, tight loop from risk to financing to settlement, integrated
funding, two-sided network effects, full control of experience.
**Constraints:** legacy and post-acquisition fragmentation, proprietary-rail incentives versus
customer-optimal routing, interoperability limits, regulatory weight.

Speaker note: this is a hypothesis, not the answer. The mirror-image claimants each have one thing the
others cannot easily take.

---

### Slide 6. The four horizons
Sequential in dependency, not in time. The industry works all four at once.
- **H0. Unify the foundation.** Fragmented infrastructure becomes reusable primitives.
- **H1. Orchestrate money movement.** Multi-rail payments, AP/AR with two-sided value, supplier
  enablement, treasury and liquidity.
- **H2. Make every transaction intelligent.** Real-time decision per transaction; embedded working
  capital from observed cash flow.
- **H3. Make financial workflows agentic.** Software executes AP/AR/treasury with scoped authority,
  auditability, settlement guarantees.

---

### Slide 7. The through-line
H0 makes H1 affordable. H1 gives H2 more surfaces to be intelligent about. H2 makes H3 safe by turning
software decisions into governed, priced, auditable events. Skip H0 and everything above is expensive.
Skip H2 and H3 is reckless.

---

### Slide 8. The experience it converges toward
**The best payment experience will not expose more financial complexity. It will absorb more of it.**
Customer sees: *"pay this invoice."*
Underneath: supplier preference, rail, funding source, FX, fraud, identity, approval, liquidity,
settlement, reconciliation.
Product principle: **increasing system intelligence should reduce customer cognitive load, not
increase it.**
Across: embedded SDK, ERP workflow, direct app, partner platform, CFO and treasury view, supplier
view, agent interface.

---

### Slide 9. Agentic: the real problem is not "AI will pay," it is trust
Moving from experimentation toward infrastructure. The unsolved parts: agent identity, delegated
authority, transaction limits, policy enforcement, authentication, fraud, liability, explainability,
auditability, dispute resolution, settlement certainty.
**When software starts moving money autonomously, trust becomes part of the payment product.**

---

### Slide 10. What it takes to win: own, partner, interoperate
Eight capabilities. No one participant owns all of them.
Own: unified infrastructure, multi-rail orchestration, real-time risk and decisioning, AI-native
product and engineering, the two-sided relationship.
Own or partner: balance-sheet and liquidity (charter-dependent).
Interoperate: trust, identity and delegated authority; distribution and reach.
Pattern: the differentiated core is infrastructure, decisioning and the two-sided relationship;
identity and reach are worth more shared than hoarded.

---

### Slide 11. The counter-thesis
Corporate payments may never converge on a single financial operating system. Banks, networks, fintech
infrastructure, ERP platforms, orchestrators and AI platforms may each hold a layer more or less
permanently. If so, the winning position is not owning the whole loop. It is being the **most trusted
and interoperable orchestration layer** across loops that never merge.
A serious strategy should survive either world.

---

### Slide 12. Strategic tensions (the discussion)
- Closed versus open
- Card economics versus rail neutrality
- Automation versus control
- Data advantage versus trust
- Build versus partner
- Simplicity versus complexity

---

### Slide 13. Open questions
1. Does orchestration accrue to whoever owns the rails and balance sheet, or whoever owns the
   workflow?
2. As real-time rails scale, what happens to rebate and float economics, and how fast?
3. When software moves money autonomously, where does liability sit, and who is best placed to price
   that risk?
4. Will the supplier side pay for two-sided value at scale, or is it a cost of buyer-side business?
5. How much of credible treasury genuinely needs a charter versus banking-as-a-service?
6. What consent and data-rights model lets payment data power underwriting without eroding trust?

Close: *"I'd like to hear where you think this is right, and where it isn't."*
